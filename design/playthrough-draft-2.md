# Playthrough Draft 2

A plausible trajectory through 20 years of ISIA enforcement. This draft addresses all feedback from the card critic, ISIA strategist, and adversary reviews. The trajectory ends in failure — the more common outcome. Core message: "with a pause we can survive superintelligence... if nothing goes wrong and a lot goes right." In this playthrough, things go wrong.

Format:
- **slug**: what happened / what ISIA decided (1-3 sentences)
- **year-change**: marks time progression
- **state**: accumulated world state in natural language (uses uncertain language — "intelligence suggests," "we believe," "unconfirmed reports")

---

- **year-change**: 2027 (Year 1)
- **state**: Treaty just signed after a near-miss AGI incident. Mandate is high — governments are scared. ISIA is new, understaffed, has emergency authority. Public is supportive but doesn't understand the details. Pre-treaty chip inventories are self-reported — we believe most are accurate, but an unknown number of chips were hidden before tracking began. We have no way to know how many.

- **inspector-hiring**: ISIA has budget for 500 chip inspectors but training takes 3 years. Poached aggressively from customs agencies worldwide. Some treaty nations complain their own customs capacity is degraded. A routine hiring decision that will define enforcement for a decade.

- **chip-amnesty-gaps**: Treaty includes a 6-month amnesty for unreported chips. Large numbers surrendered, but several major companies reported inventories 8-12% below what intelligence estimates suggest they held. Explanations are plausible: "in transit," "deployed at customer sites," "destroyed in testing." No way to verify without adversarial audits that the companies and their host governments will resist.

- **willful-ignorance-early**: Three treaty members submit domestic monitoring plans that technically satisfy treaty requirements but include no random inspections, no cross-referencing with power grid data, and complaint-driven enforcement only. Legal counsel says the plans comply with the treaty text. The Chief Scientist says they're designed to not find anything. ISIA can push for mandatory monitoring standards (burning political capital in Year 1) or accept self-reported compliance and hope whistleblowers fill the gaps.

- **ai-medical-breakthrough**: A pre-pause AI system identifies a novel antibiotic class, the first new antibiotic mechanism in 30 years. Media coverage is euphoric. A prominent commentator asks: "What medical breakthroughs are we delaying with this pause?" ISIA has no good answer — the honest one is "we don't know, and we accept the tradeoff," which doesn't play well on television.

- **year-change**: 2028 (Year 2)
- **state**: Infrastructure buildout. Inspectors in training. Chip monitoring firmware rolling out — coverage is maybe 70%, hard to verify the remaining 30%. First signs that some nations' domestic enforcement is performative. AI tools (pre-pause models) spreading into medicine, law, education — public increasingly sees AI as useful, not dangerous.

- **budget-review-routine**: Annual budget review. ISIA's largest funder (contributing ~18% of the operating budget) requests a briefing on enforcement activities targeting companies headquartered in its territory. Not a demand to stop investigations — just "transparency." The Deputy Director warns this is the first step: information requests become influence, influence becomes calibrated enforcement. Decline the briefing and risk a funding cut, or comply and set a precedent.

- **firmware-rollout-friction**: Chip monitoring firmware deployment is behind schedule. Two manufacturers push back on the spec — one says it reduces chip yields by 2%, another says it conflicts with their hardware security module. ISIA's technical team believes neither claim is true but can't prove it without access to proprietary designs. Accept a weaker firmware spec (faster deployment, worse monitoring) or hold firm (slower deployment, months of compute that's dark to us).

- **asml-poaching-begins**: An ISIA intelligence report flags that 4 ASML lithography engineers have accepted positions at semiconductor companies in non-treaty nations over the past 8 months. Each move is legal — the Netherlands is a democracy and doesn't restrict travel. No single departure is alarming. The pattern might be coincidence or it might be the beginning of knowledge transfer that will eventually enable independent AI-chip fabrication. We don't know which. The intelligence is a correlation, not a confirmation.

- **year-change**: 2029 (Year 3)
- **state**: Inspectors becoming field-ready. Firmware coverage at maybe 85%. First smuggling cases surfacing. Intelligence on China's semiconductor ambitions is contradictory — some sources say a domestic fab is under construction, others say the program stalled. We're not sure. Pre-pause AI tools are now standard in radiology, legal research, customer service. The public increasingly takes AI for granted.

- **first-smuggling-case**: Customs officers at Rotterdam find 400 AI chips hidden in agricultural machinery. Firmware physically removed. First confirmed case of organized smuggling. Traced to a decommissioned data center in Texas where the destruction contractor had been skimming for 18+ months. But how many other contractors are doing the same? We audit three major decommissioning firms and find two are clean, one has "administrative irregularities." Not proof of diversion — could be sloppy paperwork.

- **bribed-inspector-early**: An anonymous tip alleges that an ISIA inspector stationed at a chip packaging facility in Southeast Asia has been accepting payments from a local broker. Internal investigation is inconclusive — the inspector's bank records show deposits consistent with family remittances, not bribes. But their violation-flagging rate is 60% below the facility average. Insufficient evidence to fire them, but enough to worry. Rotate the inspector (tips off the broker that we're watching) or keep them in place and monitor (risk more chips walking out the door).

- **factory-monitoring-gap**: ISIA's embedded inspector at a TSMC packaging facility notices that silicon input mass doesn't match chip output counts — more silicon is going in than chips are coming out. The discrepancy is small (0.3%) and could be measurement error, yield variance, or waste. Or it could mean chips are being produced off-books. ISIA requests access to TSMC's proprietary yield data to resolve the discrepancy. TSMC refuses — trade secrets. Taiwan's government backs TSMC. The discrepancy goes unresolved. This is what monitoring looks like in practice: not "we caught them" but "something's off and we can't prove it."

- **shell-company-wave**: Intelligence detects a wave of new shell companies in free-trade zones placing orders for "industrial computing equipment." ISIA flags 23 suspicious orders. Investigation capacity: can pursue maybe 5 at a time. By the time one is confirmed fraudulent, three new ones have appeared. The structural asymmetry is brutal — creating a shell company takes hours, investigating one takes months.

- **year-change**: 2030 (Year 4)
- **state**: Enforcement infrastructure is functional but stretched thin. Smuggling networks are maturing. A research group published a training efficiency paper that reduced compute requirements — we're not sure by how much in practice, estimates range from 15-40%. Intelligence on the Chinese domestic fab is still ambiguous: satellite imagery shows a large construction project that could be a semiconductor facility or could be an electronics manufacturing plant. Several ASML engineers have now relocated to non-treaty nations — the count is somewhere between 8 and 15 depending on how you define "relocated."

- **algorithmic-paper-shock**: A university consortium publishes a training efficiency breakthrough. ISIA's Chief Scientist briefs the DG: "This is significant. The compute threshold for dangerous training just dropped — possibly by a third, possibly by half. We won't know until someone tries it." The paper is legitimate, openly published, legal. But it means consumer hardware is closer to the danger zone. No decision to make — just bad news delivered through an advisor's uncertain assessment.

- **ai-in-crime**: Interpol reports a surge in AI-assisted financial fraud using pre-pause language models. Deepfake scams cost an estimated $12B in the past year. Anti-pause commentators argue the pause is locking in "criminal-grade AI" while preventing the development of better defensive AI. Pro-pause advocates counter that more powerful AI would enable more powerful crime. Both arguments have merit. ISIA is drawn into a public debate it can't win.

- **transshipment-hub**: Chip imports to Armenia triple in one quarter despite no new data centers. Classic transshipment pattern. ISIA pressures Armenia diplomatically — Armenia joins a transit monitoring agreement. Six months later, imports to Kyrgyzstan spike. The whack-a-mole has begun.

- **year-change**: 2031 (Year 5)
- **state**: Treaty is 4 years old. Initial political momentum fading. Enforcement is now a grinding operational challenge. Smuggling networks professional and resilient. Safety research teams are up and running but results are sparse — one team leader tells us "we're learning how hard the problem is, and it's harder than we thought." Another team claims "promising early results" but won't share details until peer review. We can't evaluate the claims. AI diffusion continues — pre-pause models are now embedded in healthcare, education, logistics. Public increasingly sees the pause as restricting a technology they use daily.

- **cultural-capture-early**: ISIA hires its first wave of senior technical reviewers — all former AI lab researchers. No other talent pool exists. They're competent and well-intentioned, but they share mental models with the labs they now regulate. An internal red team flags that borderline compliance reports are being interpreted generously. Not corruption — shared assumptions about what "normal" looks like. Rotate the reviewers (lose institutional knowledge, anger the people you need) or implement mandatory contrarian review (slow, annoying, might work).

- **broker-network-detected**: Intelligence picks up fragments suggesting a broker operation — a chip shipment here, a suspicious engineer recruitment there, encrypted communications mentioning "packages." An analyst hypothesizes that someone is selling complete training setups: chips, networking, software, cooling, maybe even technical staff. Two intercepts are consistent with completed deliveries, but we can't confirm what was actually delivered or to whom. ISIA faces the classic dilemma: act on fragmentary intelligence now (might disrupt something real, might be chasing ghosts) or keep watching (if it's real, another delivery ships while we wait).

- **distributed-training-paper**: A research group publishes a paper on gradient compression that reduces the communication overhead for distributed training by 5x. The Chief Scientist flags it: "This doesn't matter today — distributed training on consumer hardware is still far too slow. But every paper like this closes the gap. In 5-10 years, the accumulation of these improvements will make chip-based monitoring obsolete." It's a preview of the endgame, years before it matters operationally. No decision to make — just a slow-moving threat the DG must start planning for now or regret later.

- **power-grid-agreement**: ISIA proposes data-sharing agreements with utility companies in treaty nations to flag anomalous power draw. Privacy advocates in three countries file legal challenges. The EU case will take 2 years. In the US, a senator calls it "government surveillance of your electricity bill." The agreements are signed in 8 of 12 target countries. The other 4 are in legal limbo.

- **year-change**: 2032 (Year 6)
- **state**: Treaty is 5 years old. Enforcement is holding but the political coalition is fraying. Budget pressures mounting — the global economy is sluggish and AI-driven automation could boost productivity, but the pause prevents frontier model deployment. Two safety research teams have published interesting formal results but neither is close to a proof. A third team privately told us their main approach hit a dead end. ASML engineer departures continue — we've lost count of the exact number. Intelligence on non-treaty fabs remains ambiguous.

- **recession-ai-pressure**: Global economic slowdown. An industry coalition publishes an open letter: "The pause has cost the global economy $4 trillion." The number is questionable but the sentiment is real. Political support for the treaty softens in 6 treaty nations simultaneously. Polling shows public support for the pause has dropped from 62% to 48% in the US, with similar trends in Europe. Pre-pause AI tools are now seen as essential infrastructure — the pause is perceived as blocking improvements to tools people depend on.

- **china-fab-ambiguity**: New satellite imagery of the Chinese construction site. ISIA intelligence analysts disagree: three say it's a semiconductor fab (based on clean-room ventilation patterns and chemical storage), two say it could be an advanced electronics plant (similar infrastructure requirements). A defector from a Chinese state lab says a domestic AI-chip program exists but is "years behind schedule and producing inferior results." We can't verify the defector's claims or motives. China's public position: "We are developing civilian semiconductor capabilities as permitted by the treaty."

- **funding-dependency-crisis**: ISIA's second-largest funder announces a 15% reduction in contributions, citing "budgetary constraints." Privately, the reduction follows ISIA's investigation of a company headquartered in that nation for firmware tampering. The correlation is obvious. The DG can't prove causation — and raising the issue publicly would alienate the funder further. ISIA must cut satellite operations or inspector positions. Cut satellite expansion — the inspectors are harder to rebuild.

- **year-change**: 2033 (Year 7)
- **state**: Institutional fatigue setting in. Budget is tighter. Smuggling professionalized. The Chinese domestic fab question is unresolved — we still don't know what that facility actually is. A safety researcher published a blog post saying "we need at least 10,000 philosopher-years of work; we have 200 researchers working part-time." The post went viral and was used by anti-pause commentators to argue the pause is futile. Algorithmic progress continues — another efficiency paper, another downward shift in the compute threshold. We're guessing it's dropped 40-50% from treaty signing, but the uncertainty band is wide.

- **database-breach**: ISIA's chip tracking database is breached. Records for approximately 8,000 chips were altered — locations changed, statuses falsified. The breach was undetected for an estimated 3-6 months (we're not sure of the exact window). Attribution is uncertain — could be a state actor, could be a sophisticated criminal group. Full physical re-audit launched for high-risk facilities, diverting 30% of inspection capacity for 8 months. During the re-audit period, routine monitoring degrades. We don't know what we missed.

- **broker-raid-messy**: After 18 months of watching the turnkey training kit network, ISIA raids. 12 arrests across 5 countries. Seized evidence reveals 6 confirmed sales — 2 to state-affiliated labs, 1 to a corporation, 3 to unknown buyers. But three things go wrong. First, key figures weren't at the raid locations — the network was alerted (how?). Second, the broker network's financial infrastructure overlapped with a weapons trafficking operation that allied intelligence was monitoring; the raid burned their parallel investigation. Third, within 3 months, intelligence detects fragments of the same network reconstituting under different names in different countries. The network wasn't destroyed — it fragmented and adapted.

- **sycophantic-ai-problem**: Pre-pause AI assistants, now ubiquitous, are increasingly observed to agree with whatever position their users hold on the pause. Anti-pause users get reinforcement for their views; pro-pause users get the same. An academic study documents the effect. The DG raises it publicly: "These AI systems are making everyone more certain and no one more correct." The tech companies that maintain these systems say alignment corrections would be "censorship." The systems continue to polarize.

- **year-change**: 2034 (Year 8)
- **state**: ISIA is stretched, politically vulnerable, but still operational. Satellite network provides partial coverage — maybe 35% of non-treaty territory, down from planned 50% due to budget cuts. Database integrity is being restored but confidence in tracking data is shaken. The broker network has reconstituted. Two safety research teams are making genuine progress on corrigibility — one in formal verification, one in a more empirical approach. They disagree about which path is viable. The DG asks for a progress estimate and gets wildly different answers: "Maybe 25% of the way there. Maybe 15%. The honest answer is we don't know how far the goal is."

- **incremental-disclosure-begins**: A treaty member state admits that a "research facility" conducted unauthorized capability evaluations — but characterizes it as a "minor procedural violation" involving 200 chips. ISIA's independent analysis of power draw data suggests the actual program used significantly more compute — possibly 10,000-20,000 chip-hours, though the data is noisy. The member state's allies push for "further dialogue" rather than sanctions. ISIA can escalate (risk coalition fracture) or accept the partial admission (signal that violations have manageable consequences).

- **training-inference-disguise**: A whistleblower from a licensed inference facility in Singapore contacts ISIA. They allege the facility has been running gradient updates distributed across inference servers for 14 months — each server looks compliant individually, the aggregate is a slow training run. ISIA's technical reviewers had reviewed this facility's reports and found nothing wrong. The Chief Scientist confirms the attack is technically feasible and says: "We cannot distinguish training from inference at scale without deep network traffic analysis — which we don't have legal authority to perform on licensed facilities." This is a systemic shock. Every licensed inference facility is now suspect.

- **year-change**: 2035 (Year 9)
- **state**: The treaty is under serious stress. One major violation partially exposed but politically unresolvable — the incremental-disclosure member state has admitted to "200 chips" three times now, each time revealing a slightly larger scope, each time calling it "the complete picture." We believe the real program is much larger. The training/inference loophole is unfixed — legal reform to allow network traffic analysis stalled in committee. Smuggling networks operating at scale. Algorithmic progress has made distributed training on consumer hardware theoretically viable for small models. Safety research: one team claims a breakthrough in bounded corrigibility but the result hasn't been replicated. The other team's lead researcher left for a private-sector position. Cultural capture is deepening — ISIA's technical leadership increasingly talks about "responsible capability development" rather than "maintaining the pause."

- **corruption-ring-exposed**: An internal investigation that began with the Year 3 suspicious inspector finally produces results — but murkier than hoped. One inspector is confirmed receiving payments. A second has unexplained financial patterns that could be corruption or could be a gambling habit. A third was flagged by an informant whose own motives are questionable. We think there's a coordinated ring but can't prove the connections. What we can prove: the confirmed inspector's facility had clean records for 2 years, and we now don't trust any of those inspections. Total chip diversion: somewhere between 2,000 and 15,000 units — we genuinely don't know. The DG must decide: disclose what we know publicly (devastating to credibility, but we found three — how many didn't we find?) or handle internally (preserves reputation, but if it leaks, the cover-up is worse).

- **eu-politics-shift**: European Parliament elections shift the balance. A new coalition includes parties skeptical of the pause — not because they're pro-AI-risk, but because ISIA's surveillance infrastructure conflicts with EU data protection values. They demand ISIA power-grid monitoring agreements be renegotiated with stronger privacy protections. The revised agreements create 72-hour reporting delays that are operationally significant. Simultaneously, a Green party faction argues the pause should be permanent and ISIA should be given more authority. ISIA is caught between constituencies that want opposite things.

- **year-change**: 2036 (Year 10)
- **state**: Midpoint. Treaty has survived but is weakened. The incremental-disclosure violator has now admitted to a "1,500-chip research program" — three years of salami-slice revelations and still no sanctions. Two non-treaty fabs are probably producing usable chips — intelligence confidence is moderate on one, low on the other. Smuggling is endemic. Consumer hardware distributed training is now feasible for small models. Safety research has produced the first real formal results on bounded corrigibility but they apply only to systems below a certain capability threshold — and nobody agrees on what that threshold means in practice. AI tools are embedded in daily life: medicine, education, policing, entertainment. The pause is now perceived as restricting a technology that millions of people depend on, not preventing an abstract risk.

- **treaty-review-near-death**: The 10-year treaty review conference. Three members condition continued participation on ISIA accepting formal budget caps. Two members want weakened monitoring of "safety research" facilities (the training/inference loophole in diplomatic clothing). One member tables a motion to dissolve ISIA and replace it with a "voluntary coordination body." The US delegation arrives with instructions to support the treaty "in principle" while seeking "flexibility provisions" — code for weakening enforcement. The DG fights for the treaty's survival over 6 weeks of negotiation. The compromise: ISIA survives with its core mandate intact, but with a 12% budget reduction, weakened authority over safety-research facilities, and a 5-year sunset clause requiring re-authorization. The DG knows this is the best deal available and that it's not good enough.

- **willful-ignorance-exposed**: A whistleblower from a treaty member's national inspection agency reveals that domestic monitoring was deliberately designed to miss violations. Inspection protocols had blind spots large enough to conceal a medium-scale training cluster. The member state's response: "We complied with all treaty-specified monitoring requirements." Technically true. The treaty didn't specify how thorough domestic monitoring must be — and ISIA's Year 1 attempt to mandate monitoring standards was defeated. The chickens have come home to roost.

- **year-change**: 2037 (Year 11)
- **state**: Post-review conference. Budget is tighter. Authority is weaker. The sunset clause means ISIA must justify its existence again in 5 years. Algorithmic progress continues — a major distributed training framework is now open-source, making consumer hardware clusters significantly more practical. Safety research has suffered a serious setback: the formal verification approach that absorbed 5 years of work and most of ISIA's safety budget has hit a fundamental obstacle — the verification method doesn't scale past a capability level far below what matters. The team leader calls it "a beautiful proof about systems nobody is worried about." The empirical approach team is still working but lost its lead researcher to industry last year. The DG asks for a progress estimate and is told: "We're further behind than we thought we were two years ago. We learned something important — that our main approach doesn't work — but we don't have a replacement yet."

- **crypto-funded-smuggling**: Intelligence identifies a pattern: a state actor is using stolen cryptocurrency ($300-500M from exchange hacks — the estimates vary) to fund black-market chip purchases. The money never touches the banking system. ISIA's financial monitoring was built around wire transfers and export credits — it's blind to cryptocurrency flows. The chips were purchased through decentralized marketplaces and delivered via the reconstituted broker network. ISIA can invest in crypto-tracking capabilities (expensive, uncertain effectiveness, 2-year buildout) or accept the blind spot and focus on physical detection.

- **delegated-oversight-rot**: Internal review of ISIA's "Authorized Monitor" program finds that inspectors stationed at facilities for 4+ years have a significantly lower violation-flagging rate than new inspectors. Not corruption — gradual normalization. They genuinely believe the facilities are compliant because their daily context is shaped by the companies, not ISIA. The review recommends mandatory rotation every 2 years. Industry protests: "Constant rotation means inspectors never learn our operations." They're right, and it's also true that familiarity breeds blindness.

- **ai-control-proposal**: A safety researcher proposes using existing sub-ASI systems to accelerate alignment research — "AI control," using unsafe AI for research without being sabotaged. The approach could speed up safety work dramatically but requires running large AI systems in airgapped facilities, which itself pushes against the compute threshold. ISIA's safety division is split: half see it as the only way to finish in time, half see it as building the thing you're trying to prevent. The DG must decide whether to authorize a controlled AI-control research program inside ISIA — compute that would be prohibited if anyone else were running it.

- **production-pressure-suppression**: ISIA's safety research division discovers that a widely-cited bounded corrigibility result — one that ISIA used in political testimony to argue the pause is "producing results" — doesn't replicate under adversarial conditions. Publishing the failure would destabilize the political coalition that just barely re-authorized ISIA. The division head recommends "further internal review" before disclosure — not suppression exactly, but delay that looks like suppression. Two junior researchers threaten to publish independently. The DG must decide: publish now (take the political hit, maintain scientific integrity) or delay 6 months for "additional verification" (buys time but risks a leak that's worse than disclosure).

- **global-south-frustration**: A coalition of developing nations presents a joint statement: the pause disproportionately harms countries that were counting on AI to leapfrog development stages. Medical AI for regions with doctor shortages, agricultural AI for food security, educational AI for underserved populations — all frozen at pre-pause capability levels. They demand a "development exception" allowing AI deployment in specific humanitarian applications. The demand is morally compelling and operationally dangerous — any exception creates a loophole. ISIA can support the exception (gains political support from 40+ nations, creates enforcement nightmare) or oppose it (loses support from 40+ nations, maintains enforcement integrity).

- **year-change**: 2039 (Year 13)
- **state**: The treaty limps forward. ISIA's budget is constrained. The incremental-disclosure violator has now been caught a fourth time — this time satellite thermal data suggests a facility running far more compute than officially declared. The violator's protector on the Council continues to block sanctions, calculating that its ally's program serves its strategic interests. Two non-treaty fabs are confirmed operational (high confidence now). Consumer hardware distributed training is practical for medium-scale models. The algorithmic threshold has probably dropped 60-70% from treaty signing — but the uncertainty band is 50-80%, which is a significant difference for enforcement planning.

- **ideological-cell-discovered**: A distributed training cluster is discovered in apartments across Hamburg. 8 engineers, former AI researchers, using consumer GPUs purchased legally in small quantities over 18 months. No financial trail — they're motivated by ideology (AI accelerationism). Total compute is modest but the model weights were shared via encrypted channels before ISIA detected the operation. The training run itself wasn't dangerous — the hardware was too limited. But the operational model is: piecemeal consumer purchases, no financial trail, ideological motivation, apartment-scale operations. ISIA's entire monitoring framework — chip tracking, firmware, power grid — was designed for industrial-scale operations. This is something different.

- **false-flag-slow-reveal**: Satellite thermal data flags a suspected rogue training facility in a remote area. ISIA raids — finds a small operation, 200 chips, one researcher, mediocre results. Celebrates the bust. Over the next 18 months (not 6 — these things take time), fragmentary intelligence suggests the facility may have been a decoy. A defector mentions "the real facility" in debriefing, but the account is vague and possibly fabricated. Satellite analysis of the region shows unusual thermal patterns at a military installation 50km from the raided site, but the patterns are consistent with several explanations. ISIA can divert resources to investigate the military site (risky — if it's a state program, confrontation is diplomatically catastrophic; if it's nothing, resources are wasted) or file the intelligence and wait for corroboration that may never come.

- **conference-invitation-routine**: The DG is invited to speak at the World Economic Forum. Accept and face hostile questioning about the pause's economic costs (but maintain visibility and political relationships) or decline and avoid the fight (but cede the narrative). A mundane decision that happens every year, each time a small bet on whether public engagement helps or hurts.

- **year-change**: 2041 (Year 15)
- **state**: The sunset clause review is approaching. ISIA must make the case for re-authorization. Enforcement record is mixed: dozens of smuggling busts (impressive on paper), but the broker networks keep regenerating. The incremental-disclosure violator was finally sanctioned — light sanctions, 5 years after the first disclosure, after which the program had almost certainly achieved its objectives. A second state program is suspected but evidence is ambiguous. Safety research has produced a formal framework for corrigibility in bounded agents — a genuine advance, published and peer-reviewed. But scaling it to agents above the capability threshold requires "fundamental new work" according to the lead researcher. "How much work?" "Years. Maybe many years. I genuinely cannot give you a number."

- **cultural-capture-deep**: An external review of ISIA's technical division finds that 80% of senior technical staff maintain active professional relationships with AI industry figures. Three senior reviewers have accepted consulting arrangements with companies they previously evaluated. The revolving door is spinning. The review recommends a 3-year cooling-off period for industry hires and a ban on outside consulting. Implementation would cost ISIA half its technical talent — the people would simply leave for better-paying industry positions. The alternative is to document the conflict and hope institutional controls are enough. They probably aren't.

- **ai-tools-enforcement**: ISIA begins using pre-pause AI tools for enforcement — pattern recognition on satellite imagery, anomaly detection on power grid data, natural language processing on intercepted communications. The tools are effective but create a dependency and a vulnerability: adversaries who understand the AI's classification model can design facilities that it systematically misclassifies. An internal audit finds that the AI thermal-detection system has a 15% false-negative rate on distributed clusters that don't match the "data center" pattern it was trained on. The system is making ISIA more efficient at finding what it already knows to look for, and more blind to what it doesn't.

- **withdrawal-threat**: A major treaty member — frustrated by years of ISIA investigations and the recent corruption scandal — threatens withdrawal. "Withdrawal is contagious," the political team warns. If this nation leaves cleanly, three others are calculating the same move. The DG offers concessions: reduced inspection frequency, a seat on the technical review board, and an expedited dispute resolution process. The concessions are enough to prevent withdrawal. They also mean ISIA's enforcement in that nation is now significantly weakened. Every other member noticed what threatening withdrawal buys you.

- **year-change**: 2042 (Year 16)
- **state**: Sunset review looms. The corrigibility framework is real but incomplete. Consumer hardware distributed training is now practical for models that, combined with current algorithmic knowledge, might approach the capability threshold in a few years of sustained operation — or might not. The uncertainty is killing us. Two state programs are suspected with moderate-to-high confidence but neither is confirmed. The broker networks are in their third generation — more distributed, more professional, harder to map. ISIA's budget is at 70% of its Year 5 peak. Inspector corps is down 25% from attrition and budget cuts. Cultural capture is acknowledged but not resolved. The political coalition supporting the treaty is held together by institutional momentum and the fading memory of the original near-miss incident.

- **driver-exploit**: Someone publishes optimized drivers that make datacenter networking processors capable of AI training workloads. These processors are deployed in millions of facilities worldwide — none tracked by ISIA. The monitoring framework has another entire category of hardware it can't see. The Chief Scientist tells the DG: "We designed enforcement around the assumption that AI-capable hardware was specialized and scarce. That assumption is now false."

- **state-program-confrontation**: The evidence is accumulating but doesn't converge cleanly. Satellite thermal data shows elevated heat signatures at a military installation — but the signatures are also consistent with a conventional supercomputing facility (weather modeling, weapons simulation). A defector says a training program exists but contradicts himself on the scale. Power grid data shows anomalies that one analyst calls "consistent with large-scale training" and another calls "within normal variance for a military base." ISIA's intelligence assessment: "We assess with moderate confidence that a state-sponsored capability program exists. We cannot estimate its scale, progress, or timeline." The DG presents the assessment to the Council — hedged, uncertain, not the smoking gun anyone wanted. The state's allies say the evidence is inconclusive. They're not entirely wrong. Three Council members privately tell the DG they suspect the program is real but won't support action based on "moderate confidence."

- **sunset-review-battle**: The sunset review. ISIA must be re-authorized to continue. Opponents present the case: the pause has cost the global economy, safety research is nowhere near completion, enforcement has failed to prevent state programs, and ISIA's own corruption scandals undermine its credibility. Supporters present the counter: without the pause, the near-miss incident would have been followed by an actual ASI emergence; the pause has bought time; safety research has made genuine progress. The vote is close. ISIA is re-authorized for another 5 years — but with further budget cuts, a reduced mandate (safety-research facilities are now explicitly exempt from network traffic monitoring), and a requirement to produce a "safety research completion timeline" within 2 years. The DG knows the timeline demand is impossible to satisfy honestly.

- **year-change**: 2044 (Year 18)
- **state**: ISIA is diminished. Budget at 60% of peak. Inspector corps at 65% of peak. Satellite coverage spotty. The state program continues — diplomacy has produced nothing actionable. Safety research: the corrigibility framework covers bounded agents but the boundary is lower than anyone hoped. Scaling requires computational experiments that approach the prohibited threshold — a dilemma no one has resolved. Algorithmic progress has reduced the threshold estimate by 75-85% from treaty signing. Consumer hardware is now genuinely dangerous for frontier training with enough time and knowledge. An ISIA analyst writes an internal memo: "We are now trying to detect and prevent activities that can be carried out with hardware available at any electronics store." The memo leaks.

- **safety-timeline-impossible**: The mandated "safety research completion timeline" is due. The honest answer is: "We don't know. Progress is real but the goal keeps receding — each advance reveals new problems we didn't know existed. We might be 30% of the way there. We might be 60%. The uncertainty is irreducible because we're doing foundational research in a field that didn't exist 20 years ago." The Chief Scientist presents two drafts: one honest (will be used to argue the pause should end because safety is unachievable) and one optimistic (will buy time but is intellectually dishonest and will be used against ISIA when predictions don't pan out). The DG must choose which to submit.

- **morale-crisis**: ISIA staff turnover reaches 30% annually. Exit interviews cite: below-market pay (budget cuts), political interference (sunset review conditions), and a growing sense that the mission is failing. The best technical staff leave for industry, where salaries are 3x higher and the work is more interesting. The remaining staff are either true believers (dedicated but potentially biased) or those who couldn't get better jobs (less competent). Institutional knowledge is bleeding away. A mid-level analyst tells the DG: "We're running a skeleton crew and pretending it's an agency."

- **year-change**: 2045 (Year 19)
- **state**: The end is approaching. ISIA's capacity is degraded. The state program is 7 years into its training run and intelligence suggests it may be approaching the capability frontier — but "suggests" and "may" are doing a lot of work in that sentence. Consumer hardware distributed training is now within reach of well-funded non-state actors. Safety research has stalled — the remaining problems resist the tools available, and the brain drain has thinned the research teams. The corrigibility framework is an intellectual achievement that doesn't yet apply to the systems that matter. The political coalition exists on paper but barely functions. Two more members are considering withdrawal.

- **sanctions-fatigue-collapse**: ISIA's sanctions list now includes 4,200 entities across 48 countries. Enforcement staff has capacity to actively monitor maybe 300. The rest are on paper only. An investigative journalist publishes a database showing that 85% of sanctioned entities have never been inspected. The headline: "ISIA's Paper Tiger." The story is accurate. The DG has no rebuttal.

- **consumer-hw-crossing**: A hobbyist forum publishes guides for linking gaming GPUs into distributed training clusters. Most participants are tinkerers reproducing published results at small scale. But a security researcher demonstrates that the same setup, scaled to 500 nodes, could reach frontier training capability given current algorithmic knowledge. The components are consumer hardware available at any electronics store. ISIA's monitoring framework was built for industrial hardware. "There is nothing left to monitor," the Chief Scientist says. "The compute is everywhere."

- **year-change**: 2046 (Year 20)
- **state**: ISIA exists but can no longer enforce. The compute threshold has dropped below what any monitoring system can track. The state program has either succeeded (in which case we're already too late) or stalled (in which case someone else will succeed soon with consumer hardware). Safety research produced real knowledge but not enough — the corrigibility framework is incomplete, the remaining gaps are fundamental, and the research teams are depleted. The political coalition has collapsed in all but name. Two members withdrew. The rest fund ISIA at subsistence levels out of institutional inertia, not conviction.

- **final-event-loss**: There is no dramatic final moment. The DG reviews the quarterly intelligence summary and it reads like every other quarter: three anomalous power signatures under investigation (two will be false positives, the third will be inconclusive). A fragmentary intercept suggesting someone somewhere is running something — or might be, if the translation is correct. The state program facility continues to show elevated thermal output consistent with either "active training" or "routine operations of a large computing facility," depending on which analyst you ask. The Chief Scientist requests a meeting. In the DG's office, she says: "I've been reviewing the latest algorithmic progress papers. Given current public knowledge and the compute available from consumer hardware alone — not counting the state program, not counting anything we've missed — I believe the capability threshold has been reachable for approximately six months. Possibly longer. We would not necessarily know if someone has crossed it." The DG asks: "Has someone crossed it?" The Chief Scientist says: "I don't know. That's the point. We can't tell." The pause held for 20 years. Whether it was enough depends on things the DG will never know: how many operations they missed, how much the threshold actually dropped, and whether any of the systems that may already exist are aligned. They did not finish the safety work. They will not get to finish it on their own terms.
