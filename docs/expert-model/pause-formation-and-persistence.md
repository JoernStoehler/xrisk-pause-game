# Pause formation and persistence

## Founding condition

The project's mainline scenario begins with the United States and China already signed onto an international pause and the player appointed Director-General. This is a pedagogical conditioning choice, not a forecast that treaty formation is easy or imminent. It moves play toward the underexplored question: what a serious pause must physically and institutionally do.

## From agreement to control

A founding agreement does not itself stop dangerous computation. Early policy lies on several separable axes:

- voluntary cessation, legal prohibition, or direct technical stop authority;
- company ownership, state ownership, or ISIA custody and factual control;
- on-site monitors, telemetry, credentials, power control, component removal, relocation, or destruction;
- permissions for training, fine-tuning, evaluation, inference, and safety research;
- evidence requirements and enforcement latency;
- domestic versus reciprocal or cross-national inspection;
- compensation, due process, and reversibility.

Immediate placement of monitors at known frontier sites can close a large, urgent part of the surface before universal registration and consolidation finish. It does not solve covert clusters, military systems, distributed work, supply-chain reconstruction, or ambiguity over permitted inference.

## Persistence is produced by several mechanisms

No single “treaty cohesion” variable explains survival. Restraint may persist because of shared catastrophe beliefs, reciprocal verification, domestic law, sunk institutional capacity, beneficiary constituencies, professional norms, reputational costs, material coercion, or simple lack of a feasible evasion route. These mechanisms respond differently to crises and interventions.

Likewise, failure has distinct pathways:

1. **Belief change:** leaders or institutions cease to expect superintelligence or catastrophe.
2. **Preference change or aggregation:** actors accept the risk, or immediate interests dominate a diffuse future loss.
3. **Exemption and scope erosion:** military, intelligence, safety, or economic exceptions grow until the prohibition is porous.
4. **Implementation failure:** authority exists but staffing, access, technical competence, or domestic enforcement does not.
5. **Capture and information failure:** evaluators, advisors, legislatures, or implementing agencies filter evidence or adopt a misleading consensus.
6. **False higher-order belief:** each side prefers restraint but expects the other to exploit it, producing preemption or unwillingness to spend political capital.
7. **Crisis displacement:** a Taiwan conflict or another salient dispute activates goals and heuristics that displace ASI risk in operational decisions.
8. **Organizational discontinuity:** inspectors evacuate, communication closes, delegated military authority activates, or physical custody fragments even while top leaders retain their beliefs.

These pathways can interact, but they should not be narrated as interchangeable losses of “political health.”

## What historical treaty research contributes

Arms-control, nonproliferation, environmental, and international-organization research contributes candidate mechanisms:

- verification can reduce uncertainty without producing trust;
- reciprocal access may be politically harder than technically useful;
- issue linkage can supply bargains or destroy cooperation;
- legalization and delegation trade flexibility against credibility and sovereignty;
- domestic constituencies and policy feedback can stabilize a regime;
- organizational routines can sustain performance or normalize deviance;
- coercion can enforce compliance while damaging legitimacy or inducing substitution;
- crises can interrupt operations independently of formal withdrawal.

Cases such as the JCPOA, INF and Open Skies treaties, DPRK negotiations, Montreal Protocol, and Paris Agreement are useful for process tracing, warning signs, and rival explanations. None supplies a direct annual failure rate for a US–China ASI pause because the stakes, detection technology, actor incentives, and speed differ radically.

At current granularity, the proactive compute-control frame is attributed to `MIRI-TREATY-01`; historical mechanism families to `TREATY-PROCESS-01` and `SOCIAL-MECHANISMS-01`; the frontier-specific failure branches to `JORN-REVIEW-2026-07-CONTROL`; and their integration to `ATLAS-SYNTHESIS-01`. ## Intervention logic

A useful intervention description traces:

`proposal → legal authority → required approvals → implementation capacity → physical implementation → target adaptation → observable evidence → actor interpretation → political response`

This prevents three common mistakes: treating policy announcement as implementation, treating detected evidence as ground truth, and treating “the state” as one actor.

## Principal disagreements to preserve

1. Whether a serious pause can permit broad existing-model inference without predictably eroding its own thresholds.
2. Which control package is the minimum robust starting point: monitoring, custody, relocation/consolidation, component disablement, or destruction.
3. Whether cross-inspection is politically indispensable for mutual belief or so sovereignty-costly that strong internal monitoring is more durable.
4. How often a leader who explicitly internalizes near-term extinction risk nevertheless chooses an available pause-weakening action, rather than apparent cases decomposing into changed belief, limited control, organizational action, or ordinary error.
5. How war affects restraint when both top leaderships retain a shared catastrophe belief.

## Pruning guidance for the game

Preserve asymmetric actor maps, authority chains, physical access, sensor and interpretation failures, rival hypotheses, and several distinct crisis pathways. Aggregate routine domestic politics when it does not change these. Do not infer a historical base rate and apply it as an annual random collapse probability.
