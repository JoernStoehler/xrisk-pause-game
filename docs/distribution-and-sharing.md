## Audiences and use contexts

The likely early audience is people who already grant, or are open to granting, a severe AI risk premise and want to reason about whether a pause can work. The July synthesis, “Audience segments and channel fit,” names PauseAI and adjacent organizers; EA, rationalist, and alignment readers; AI governance, security, and policy educators; and premise-qualified technical practitioners. These groups differ in what they can use. An organizer may want a short discussion object for a chapter or official meeting. A policy educator may need a case with a facilitator guide and explicit sources. A technical practitioner may test whether the assumed control path is credible. A curious solo player may want a satisfying game first. The synthesis ranks these groups by **project judgment**, not measured demand or market size. Its technical-author risk survey and broad US regulation survey qualify possible conversations; neither measures support for this treaty or willingness to play.

Three distribution forms remain live: a self-contained mobile game encountered through links or game channels; a compact open-web explorable or paired-case explanation shared in discussions; and a facilitated classroom, workshop, or briefing case. They may be separate products if their audiences require incompatible pacing and depth. The old `docs/product/PRODUCT_ARCHITECTURE_PORTFOLIO.md`, “Shape portfolio,” compares integrated, separate, and layered forms as conditional hypotheses. A voluntary game's engagement cannot be inferred from workshop attendance, and a workshop's policy usefulness cannot be inferred from game storefront traffic.

## What might travel

A shareable object should carry a concrete reasoning question or consequence that another person can inspect: for example, how the same formal order yielded different physical control, or how the same signal warranted different responses. That is a **design hypothesis** from `docs/research/AUDIENCE_AND_DISTRIBUTION.md`, “Distribution hypotheses,” and `docs/game-design/README.md`, “Replay and sharing.” The old proposals for after-action headlines, same-world replays, cards, and advisors are optional implementations. Shared text must not advertise a survival score, diagnostic fixture frequency, unapproved sufficiency claim, or a false impression that one policy always wins. A facilitator may instead share a case sheet and discussion prompt; a technical reviewer may share a trace and source note.

Pleasure, learning, and spread interact but require separate verdicts. A game people enjoy may be easier to recommend but teach little. A terse explorable may teach well and spread only inside specialist networks. A facilitated case may reach fewer people but be reused in decisions that matter. The July ledger's *Darfur is Dying* study associates emotion and sharing in one older college sample, and its facilitated-game case reports reach in a different topic; neither predicts this artifact's referrals or influence. The old `docs/product/SUCCESS_MODEL.md`, “Failure modes and proxy traps,” correctly flags the risk of treating session length, share intent, or community size as mission success; that is a product reasoning constraint, not a measured effect here.

## How to learn whether a route works

For each channel, keep a distinct chain: exposure → premise-qualified start → meaningful engagement → causal learning/transfer → referral or repeated use → observed policy-context use. Ask how the recipient arrived and which use context they were in. A share-button press is only intent; a referred visit is only exposure. For a voluntary game, observe whether recipients start and reach a substantive decision without coaching. For a facilitated case, observe preparation cost, whether the facilitator can run it accurately, whether participants reason about a novel case, and whether anyone independently requests reuse. For policy-facing use, record the actual teaching, briefing, or design task and its constraints; do not infer influence from attendance. These tests follow `docs/review/REVIEW_ROUTING.md`, “Sharing and distribution,” and remain proposals until real recipients are observed.

The view changes if a low-cost discussion artifact produces comparable transfer and more qualified reuse than a game; if a solo game earns voluntary referrals across audience groups; or if facilitators are the only credible route to comprehension and repeated use. Channel comparisons should preserve audience and context rather than pool them into a virality score. The July source scan leaves current channel activity, access to educators, audience denominators, actual sharing behavior, and institutional adoption unverified. A future targeted check should refresh those facts and test one credible artifact with recipients before scaling outreach.
